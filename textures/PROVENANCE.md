# Provenance

The carpet patterns' source patents are US design patents from 1882-1884: six name
Eugene A. Crowe of Boston as inventor, D 13,108 and D 13,109 name Edwin Fisher of Boston, and D 13,110 names John Fisher of New York.
Comp III (`us-d268339`) is a 1983 patent, covered below. Design patents of that era ran 3.5, 7 or 14 years, so
all had expired by 1898 at the latest: the patents are in the public domain.
The drawing sheets are the USPTO / Google Patents scans (the 12,657 scan is
stamped "Best Available Copy").

Links: every carpet patent links to its Google Patents page. 14,685 and 12,657 were opened and confirmed; the 13,102, 13,483, 13,105, 13,106, 13,108, 13,109 and 13,110 links were supplied by the site owner and not opened here (no network access in the build session). For 13,102, 13,105, 13,106, 13,108, 13,109, 13,110, 13,483 and 12,657 the site owner also supplied the patent PDFs, which were read (see below); the 14,685 PDF was supplied later and read too (see below).

"Same inventor" here means the name on the drawing sheet. The 1884 and 1882
(12,657) specifications name the Lowell Manufacturing Company as assignee, and so do
the 13,102, 13,105, 13,106, 13,108, 13,109, 13,110 and 13,483 specifications (read from the PDFs).

| `switchback-trail` | none | n/a | Procedural | Written by `tools/make_procedural.py trail` (800 px mask, ink coverage 0.8%). **Routed over the same terrain as `topo-contours`** (both call one function, `_terrain`), so layered at the same size each loop lies on the hills it was routed over. Three closed loops, each a climb and a return. *Climb:* from the foot of a steep, straight face (a fall line of 7.5 contour intervals; feet at (420, 160), (40, 420) and (330, 740), found with `trail --find`) to its top, as the least-cost route on a 4 px grid with 16 headings, **never steeper than a grade of 0.0042 per px** (43% of the terrain's median slope, 0.0097) and **confined to a corridor 60 px either side of the fall line**: the grade limit forces it to traverse across the face along the contours, and the corridor forces it to double back, which is what makes switchbacks. Cost = distance, plus penalties for side slope and for grade, plus **a turning penalty** (100 px for a 180 degree turn, quadratic in the angle). *Return:* the cheapest route back with a steeper limit (0.0068, since riders descend steeper than they climb) that keeps 30 px clear of the climb except at its ends. Routes are then reduced to their legs (Douglas-Peucker, 6 px) and every corner is rounded with a 6 px tangent arc. **How it got here (three rejected versions):** (1) vertical zigzag columns with rounded hairpins, drawn without reference to the terrain: rejected, because a trail that ignores the contours does not make sense; (2) a steering simulation that followed the local slope at a fixed grade and flipped direction after set leg lengths: it wobbled with every ripple of the terrain and read as a wandering river; (3) a plain least-cost route across the tile, which threads the saddles like a game trail but has no switchbacks, because a least-cost route simply avoids steep ground. The corridor climb is what produces switchbacks. **Without the turning penalty** the cheapest climb is a saw-tooth of 16-20 tiny legs, because every zigzag that climbs at the limit costs the same length whatever its leg size. **What the numbers say:** loops of 998, 801 and 802 px (59, 47 and 47 dashes of about 17 px). Over the whole trail 55% of the climb's length runs within 30 degrees of the contour direction (mean angle 33 degrees) against 33% and 45 degrees for random headings; the return is 37% and 39 degrees (it is deliberately steeper). The rest of the climb is mostly the hairpin turns, which must cross the contours, and over 8 px windows the grade there exceeds the limit (maximum 0.011-0.014) for the same reason; the corridor legs run at about the limit (median 0.004). It is a plausible line, not a surveyed one: no real map, elevation data or trail was used. Dashes (3.2 px stroke, 11 px visible, period 17 px) are drawn one by one, not with an SVG `stroke-dasharray`: a dash array on a path with arcs puts dashes where the renderer's own length estimate says, which was about 1 px off per tile in Chromium and differs between browsers. The period is chosen per loop so a whole number of dashes closes it. **Seam check:** the mask tiled 3 x 3 against every loop drawn at every wrapped offset on a larger canvas: 19 of 5.76 million pixels differ by more than 0.1 alpha (maximum 0.125), spread evenly over the three tile rows. The loop from (40, 420) crosses the left and right edges (52 px of ink within 6 px of the right edge), so the wrapped copies are exercised; no loop touches the top or bottom edge. |

## Seam checks

Each tile was checked by comparing its right edge to its left and its bottom to
its top (correlation, at 1/8 scale) against the correlation between neighboring
columns, and by viewing the tile shifted half its width and height with
wraparound. Every adapted tile shows no straight seam in that view. Two caveats: the grid
lines can look doubled where a repair cut crosses them, and the repaired
swatches (13,102 especially) have their central motif partly re-cut.

## US D 268,339 (Comp III)

The tread design of the Tioga Comp III tire (the tire identification is from the
site owner; the patent page lists no tire name). Inventors Syoji Inae and Eiji
Fukuchi; assigned to Mitsuboshi Belting Ltd. (assignment recorded 1982-10-28);
application US 06/349,941 filed 1982-02-18; granted 1983-03-22; expired 1997-03-22
([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/USD268339S/en)). The patent
title was not recorded. The tile was checked by eye shifted half its width and
height with wraparound; no seam shows. The scan has rough, slightly fuzzy edges,
which survive into the mask.
